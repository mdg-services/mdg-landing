## What density at 15°C means

Density tells you how heavy a fixed volume of fuel is. MDG 2024 gives it in kg/m³, kilograms per cubic metre. Petrol is lighter than diesel, so a petrol reading is lower than a diesel one {{7.6}}. The printed limits are on the [density range page](@density-range).

Why 15°C? Fuel expands when it warms and shrinks when it cools. One fuel reads a lower density on a hot afternoon than on a cold morning. A fixed temperature makes readings comparable. That reason is a general explanation, not MDG text. What MDG does say is to state the density at 15°C {{1.2(iv)}} {{1.2(xiv)}} {{1.4.1(c)}}.

You read the density on a hydrometer and the temperature on a thermometer. Then you convert the density to 15°C with the ASTM density conversion table. MDG names Table 53B for the check on a tanker {{1.2(iv)}}. It does not print the table. Do not convert by eye. Use the table, or the [15°C calculator](@tools-density-at-15).

## Three times you measure density, and one thing to keep ready

| When | What you do | The standard |
|---|---|---|
| A tanker arrives | Sample each compartment after draining about 20 litres from its manifold. Read density and temperature. Convert to 15°C {{1.2(viii)}} | Within ±3.0 kg/m³ of the invoice density {{1.2(iv)}} |
| After decanting | Run at least 50 litres through the pump, then sample from the nozzle and record the density at 15°C {{1.2(xiv)}} | Entered in the density register |
| Every morning | Sample from the pump nozzle and record the opening density at 15°C before the first sale {{1.4.1(c)}} | By 10 AM at the latest {{5.1.9}} |
| Always (kit, not a reading) | Keep your calibrated hydrometers, thermometers and the ASTM table ready {{1.5(vi)}} | Working, and available when an inspector asks |

### 1. On receipt, against the invoice

The supply location writes the density at 15°C on the invoice, with the filling point, the storage tank number and the release time {{2.2.1(iv)}}. It checks the density at the filling point every two hours and whenever it switches tanks {{3.1(h)}}.

When the tanker arrives, the dealer matches the invoice with the lorry. The recorded density at 15°C is one of the items {{1.1(v)}}. Each compartment's 15°C reading is then checked against that figure, and must be within ±3.0 kg/m³ {{1.2(iv)}} {{1.2(viii)}}. See the [invoice density check, step by step](@density-invoice-check) and the [full receipt checklist](@receiving-a-tanker).

### 2. After decanting

Once the load is in the tank, run at least 50 litres through the pump. That pushes out the product left in the line. Then sample from the nozzle. The reading is the density of the mixed product in the tank {{1.2(xiv)}}. It is the reference density for samples taken from your outlet afterwards {{2.10}}.

### 3. Every morning

Draw a sample from the pump nozzle and record the opening density at 15°C before you start selling {{1.4.1(c)}}. The latest time is 10 AM {{5.1.9}}. Which figure an inspector asks for depends on the time of the visit and on whether a load came. See [the reference density rule](@density-reference-10am).

### 4. The kit you keep ready

This one is not a reading. MDG does not ask the dealer to measure density on request. It asks the dealer to have calibrated hydrometers and thermometers, the ASTM density conversion table and any other testing equipment that applies. They have to work, and an inspector can ask to see them at any time {{1.5(vi)}}.

The 20.03.2025 Minimum Service Obligations circular also lists the availability of a "Q&Q kit" (item 14) {{circular}}. It does not say what is in the kit. Ask your oil company whether it includes this equipment. MDG also asks the dealer to keep filter paper for checking petrol: see the [filter paper test](@density-filter-paper) {{1.4.1(a)}}.

## What an inspector compares against

MDG sets the reference density in two stages {{2.10}}.

| Stage | The reference figure |
|---|---|
| Until the tanker is decanted | The density recorded in the dispatch document. The supply location records the density at 15°C on the invoice {{3.1(h)}} |
| After decanting | The density of the mixed product in the tank, recorded after at least 50 litres went through the pump |

A lab sample must fall within the permissible (reproducibility) limit of the test method when compared with the reference sample {{2.10}}. The sampling chapter does not print a kg/m³ figure for that limit.

At the outlet, a mobile lab's test report has three lines for density: your tank's density after the last receipt, the density the officer observes, and the variation between the two. The maximum variation printed is ±3.0 kg/m³ {{annexure 12a}} {{annexure 13a}}. The label on a sample drawn from your outlet puts the density "as recorded in the Density register" next to the density the officer observed {{annexure 4}}. Your own register entry is the reference {{2.10}}.

An IndianOil display board tells customers that the allowed variation from your recorded reference density is ±3.0 kg/m³ at 15°C. That is the oil company's wording, not an MDG clause.

> **Note:** good fuel does not cure a missing record. If the right figure is missing and the lab sample passes, you still face the penalty for the missing figure {{5.1.9}}.

## The density register

MDG asks for the density records to be kept in the density register, in the format of Annexure 1 {{1.2(xiv)}}. Keep it for the current and previous financial years {{5.1.12}}. Missing records are an irregularity. It is major if records since the last inspection are missing {{8.3}}, and minor if only older ones are {{8.4}}. See the [density register format with a filled example](@density-register).

## Displaying density

MDG lists non-display of density with other display items, such as the opening stock of the day, the zero-before-delivery sticker on each pump and the product name on each nozzle {{5.1.17}}. These are minor irregularities {{8.4}}. The first time, the action is a warning-cum-guidance letter. The second time it is Rs 10,000 per irregularity. From the third time it is Rs 25,000 per irregularity. The miscellaneous minor items listed under 8.4 (a) to (h), found together, count as one irregularity for action {{8.4}}.

MDG does not say which density figure the board must show, or in what format. The stock board in an oil company's display-board list has a density column next to stock and rate for each product. That list is not MDG text. Ask your oil company which reading it wants shown. See [every board MDG requires](@service-display-boards).

## When density goes wrong

| Situation | What MDG says |
|---|---|
| The reference density an inspector needs is missing | Sales and supplies of all products stop at once and samples go to the lab. If the sample passes, it is a major irregularity: 15 days' suspension, then 30 days, then termination. If it fails, the action is the one for adulteration {{5.1.9}} {{8.3}} |
| A density or filter paper test fails | The oil company officer suspends sales and supplies of all products. Samples are drawn and sent to the lab on priority {{2.5(K)}} |
| A mobile lab finds the product fails on density or on any other clinical test | The oil company acts for established adulteration {{7.4}}. Adulteration is a critical irregularity, with termination at the first instance {{8.2}} |
| A lab sample is outside the permissible limits when compared with the reference sample | That falls within MDG's definition of adulteration {{5.1.1}} {{8.2}} |
| Density is not displayed | Minor irregularity {{8.4}} |

MDG uses the words "density failure" without saying which reading counts as one {{2.2.2.2}}. Ask your oil company. Instances are counted over five years {{8.5.5}}. See [how repeat offences are counted](@penalties-repeat-and-unpaid).

This is general information. For a notice or a penalty in your own case, speak to your oil company and to a lawyer.
