## What density at 15°C means

The Marketing Discipline Guidelines 2024 (MDG 2024) are the oil companies' rules for their dealers under the dealership agreement. They use density as one check on fuel quality, and they ask the dealer to measure and record it at set moments.

Density is the weight of a fixed volume of fuel. MDG 2024 states it in kg/m³, kilograms per cubic metre. Petrol is the lighter fuel, so a petrol reading comes out lower than a diesel one {{7.6}}. The limits MDG prints are on the [density range page](@density-range).

Why 15°C? Fuel swells when it warms and shrinks when it cools, so the same fuel reads a lower density on a hot afternoon than on a cold morning. Quoting every reading at one temperature makes readings comparable. That is a general explanation, not MDG's wording. What MDG asks is that the density is stated at 15°C {{1.2(iv)}} {{1.2(xiv)}} {{1.4.1(c)}}.

The dealer reads density on a hydrometer and temperature on a thermometer, then converts the density to 15°C with the ASTM density conversion table. For the tanker check, MDG names Table 53B {{1.2(iv)}}. It does not print the table, so do not convert by eye. Use the table, or the [15°C calculator](@tools-density-at-15).

## Three measurements and one set of equipment

| When | What the dealer does | What it is held to |
|---|---|---|
| A tanker arrives | Runs off about 20 litres at each compartment's manifold, then takes a sample from that compartment. Reads density and temperature and converts to 15°C {{1.2(viii)}} | Within ±3.0 kg/m³ of the invoice density {{1.2(iv)}} |
| After the load is in the tank | Runs at least 50 litres through the pump, takes a nozzle sample and notes the 15°C density {{1.2(xiv)}} | Goes into the density register |
| Every morning | Takes a nozzle sample and notes the 15°C density before the first sale {{1.4.1(c)}} | No later than 10 AM {{5.1.9}} |
| At all times (equipment, not a reading) | Holds calibrated hydrometers, thermometers and the ASTM table {{1.5(vi)}} | In working order, and shown when an inspector asks |

### 1. When a tanker arrives

The supply location writes the 15°C density on the invoice, together with the filling point, the number of the tank the lorry was filled from and the time it was released {{2.2.1(iv)}}. It checks density at the filling point every two hours, and again whenever it switches tanks {{3.1(h)}}.

The dealer matches the invoice to the lorry on arrival, and the recorded 15°C density is one of the points matched {{1.1(v)}}. Each compartment's own 15°C reading must then fall within ±3.0 kg/m³ of the invoice figure {{1.2(iv)}} {{1.2(viii)}}. Next: [the invoice density check, step by step](@density-invoice-check) and [the full receipt checklist](@receiving-a-tanker).

### 2. After decanting

The pipe between the tank and the nozzle still holds the product from before the delivery. Run at least 50 litres through the pump to clear it, then take the nozzle sample. That reading stands for the mixed fuel now in the tank {{1.2(xiv)}}. It is the reference density for samples drawn from your outlet from then on {{2.10}}.

### 3. Every morning

Before the first sale, take a nozzle sample and note its density at 15°C {{1.4.1(c)}}. The latest time is 10 AM {{5.1.9}}. Which figure an inspector asks for depends on when the visit happens and on whether a tanker has been unloaded that day. See [the reference density rule](@density-reference-10am).

### 4. The equipment to keep ready

This one is equipment, not a reading. MDG asks the dealer to hold calibrated hydrometers and thermometers, whatever other test equipment applies, and the ASTM density conversion table, to keep them in working order and to show them to inspecting authorities when asked {{1.5(vi)}}.

The 20.03.2025 minimum service obligations circular, a common format for the dealers of all three oil companies, also lists having a "Q&Q kit" available (item 14) {{circular}}. It does not list what the kit holds. Ask your oil company whether it covers this equipment. MDG separately asks the dealer to keep filter paper for petrol: see [the filter paper test](@density-filter-paper) {{1.4.1(a)}}.

## What an inspector compares against

MDG fixes the reference density for a sample in two stages {{2.10}}.

| Stage | Reference figure |
|---|---|
| Until the lorry is decanted | The density in the dispatch document. The supply location writes it on the invoice at 15°C {{3.1(h)}} |
| After decanting | The density of the mixed product in the tank, noted after at least 50 litres went through the pump |

A lab result on a sample is compared with the reference sample, and the gap must stay inside the reproducibility limit of the test method {{2.10}}. MDG's sampling chapter does not give that limit as a kg/m³ figure.

Two forms carry density too. A mobile lab's test report has a density section with three lines: your tank's density after the last receipt, the reading the officer takes, and the gap between them. The largest gap printed is 3.0 kg/m³ {{annexure 12a}} {{annexure 13a}}. The label on a sample drawn from your outlet carries your register density beside the officer's reading {{annexure 4}}. Your own register entry is therefore the reference {{2.10}}.

> **Note:** good fuel does not cure a missing record. If the right figure is missing and the lab sample passes, the penalty for the missing figure still applies {{5.1.9}}.

## The density register

MDG asks for density records to be kept in a density register, following the format in Annexure 1 {{1.2(xiv)}}, for the present financial year and the one before it {{5.1.12}}. Missing records are an irregularity. It is major if the records since the last inspection are missing {{8.3}}, and minor if those are there but older ones are not {{8.4}}. See [what the density register should hold, with an illustration](@density-register).

## Displaying density

MDG lists not displaying the density among its minor irregularities, together with other display items such as the opening stock of the day {{5.1.17}} {{8.4}}. It does not say which density figure the board should show or in what form. The oil company may give you a list of the boards and what each carries. Confirm the current list with it. See [the boards MDG requires](@service-display-boards).

For minor irregularities, MDG sets the minimum action at a warning-cum-guidance letter the first time, Rs 10,000 per irregularity the second time and Rs 25,000 per irregularity from the third time {{8.4}}. Four points go with those amounts:

- The amounts are the minimum. The oil company may take higher action, up to termination {{8.5.1}}.
- No action can follow until the irregularity has been established {{8.5.2}}.
- Instances are counted over five years, and that count includes instances under the previous guideline {{8.5.5}}.
- When several things are found at one visit, two clauses speak to it. 8.4 says that the miscellaneous items listed under (a) to (h) count as one irregularity for action {{8.4}}. 8.5.3 says that irregularities found together are each counted as an instance, with a compounding effect {{8.5.3}}. MDG does not say how the two fit together. Ask your oil company.

## When density goes wrong

| Situation | What MDG says |
|---|---|
| The reference figure an inspector needs is missing | Sales and supplies of every product stop at once and samples go to the lab. If the sample passes, it is a major irregularity: suspension for 15 days the first time, 30 days the second time, termination the third time. If it fails, the action is the one for adulteration {{5.1.9}} {{8.3}} |
| A density or filter paper test fails | The oil company officer suspends sales and supplies of every product and sends samples to the lab on priority {{2.5(K)}} |
| A mobile lab finds the product failing on density or on any other clinical test | The oil company acts for established adulteration {{7.4}}. Adulteration is a critical irregularity, with termination at the first instance {{8.2}} |
| A lab sample falls outside the permissible limits against the reference sample | This fits MDG's definition of adulteration {{5.1.1}} {{8.2}} |
| Density is not displayed | Minor irregularity {{8.4}} |

The same qualifiers apply: the amounts are the minimum and the oil company may take higher action {{8.5.1}}, an irregularity must be established first {{8.5.2}}, and the five-year count includes the previous guideline {{8.5.5}}. MDG refers to a "density failure" without saying which reading counts as one {{2.2.2.2}}. Ask your oil company. See [how repeat offences are counted](@penalties-repeat-and-unpaid).
