## What the rules check instead of high or low

MDG sets a range for each fuel, then asks three questions. None asks for a higher or a lower number.

1. **Is the sample inside the range at 15°C?** That is the specification a lab tests against {{7.6}}.
2. **Does the tanker match its invoice?** The 15°C reading must be within ±3.0 kg/m³ of the invoice density {{1.2(iv)}}. See the [tanker density check](@density-invoice-check).
3. **Is your own record on file?** An inspector measures a sample from your outlet against the density you recorded for the tank {{2.10}}, and a missing record is an irregularity by itself {{5.1.9}}. See the [10 AM reference density](@density-reference-10am).

| Fuel | Range at 15°C (kg/m³) | A reading outside it |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol (MS), every BS VI grade MDG names | 720–775 {{7.6}} | Not to specification. Read both instruments again and convert again |
| Diesel (HSD), BS VI up to B7 | 810–845 {{7.6}} | Not to specification. For fuel from Assam crude, Government of India notices may relax the range; ask your oil company {{7.6}} |
| Diesel blended with biodiesel, B8–B20 | 820–860 {{7.6}} | Not to specification. Check which blend you were supplied |

A mobile lab sample that fails on density brings the action for established adulteration {{7.4}}. The standards behind each range are on the [density range page](@density-range).

## A lower reading is not a worse fuel

The same fuel reads lower on a hot afternoon than on a cool morning. For example (a made-up sample, worked out with the [15°C calculator](@tools-density-at-15)): a petrol of 753.1 kg/m³ at 15°C reads 742.5 on the hydrometer at 27°C. Only the temperature changed, not the fuel. Convert before you judge a reading: see [why density changes with temperature](@density-temperature).
