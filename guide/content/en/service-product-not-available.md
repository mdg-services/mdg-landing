## What the circular says, and what it leaves out

Point 1 asks the dealer to always ensure all products are available at the outlet. The inspector marks it YES or NO, and a NO is acted on under 5.1.18 or the respective clause {{circular}}.

MDG and the circular do not say:

- how early to order;
- what counts as dry: one nozzle, one tank or one grade;
- whether a late delivery from the oil company is an excuse.

Ask your oil company on each. MDG 2024 uses the word "dry" in one place only: measuring a shortage when a tanker arrives at an empty outlet {{1.2(xi)}}. See [short dip and water](@tanker-short-dip-water).

General practice, not an MDG rule: order before stock runs low, and write down the date and time of each order, so you can show when you asked.

## Two very different outcomes

| Situation | Class | Minimum action | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| The product runs out (point 1 marked NO) | Minor | A warning-cum-guidance letter, then Rs 10,000, then Rs 25,000 per irregularity | {{circular}} {{5.1.18}} {{8.4(iii)(f)}} |
| Product bought from a source the oil company has not authorised, such as another dealer | Critical | Termination at the first instance | {{1.5(iii)}} {{5.1.6}} {{8.2(vi)}} {{8.2}} |

{{penalty-note}}

Before termination for a critical irregularity goes ahead, the dealership's signatories or their nominees get a personal hearing {{8.6}}. See [unauthorised purchase and sale](@penalties-unauthorised-purchase-sale).

## An example

For example (a made-up outlet): diesel runs out at 6 PM on a Saturday, and a neighbouring dealer offers to sell 2,000 litres. Taking it is a purchase from a source other than the principal oil company {{1.5(iii)}} {{5.1.6}}, which is critical {{8.2(vi)}}. Leaving the pump dry until the oil company's tanker arrives can be marked a point 1 NO, which is minor {{circular}} {{5.1.18}}.
