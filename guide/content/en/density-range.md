## Petrol pump me density kitni honi chahiye?

The Marketing Discipline Guidelines 2024 (MDG 2024) are the oil companies' rules for their dealers under the dealership agreement. Their density limits, all at 15°C, are {{7.6}}:

| Fuel | Density at 15°C, kg/m³ | Standard to check |
|---|---|---|
| Petrol (MS): grades MG, E5, E10, E12, E15 (BS VI) | 720–775 | IS 2796 and IS 17586 |
| Petrol (MS): grade E20 (BS VI) | 720–775 | IS 17021 |
| Diesel (HSD): BS VI up to B7 | 810–845 | IS 1460 |
| Diesel blended with biodiesel: B8 to B20 | 820–860 | IS 16531 |

The standards are revised from time to time, and MDG says a change in a BIS specification or test method applies with immediate effect {{7.6}}. Check the current edition of each standard, or ask your oil company, before you rely on a figure. MDG's test tables also cover appearance, colour and distillation, and viscosity for diesel. We do not reproduce them here. Read them in the guideline itself.

E5 to E20 are petrol blended with ethanol {{annexure 7}}. MDG treats motor spirit and motor gasoline as one product {{7.6}}, and petrol is the everyday name. The same density figures also appear in the lab test list and in the mobile lab test reports {{annexure 5}} {{annexure 12a}} {{annexure 13a}}.

## Your own test is not this range

The range is what a lab measures a sample against. A mobile lab runs several clinical tests on the nozzle sample, not only density: appearance, colour and distillation for both petrol and diesel, and viscosity at 40°C for diesel as well {{7.4}}. They run whatever the density result is. A product that fails on density or on any other clinical test is dealt with as established adulteration {{7.4}}.

The checks you make yourself are different.

- **On receipt:** each compartment, converted to 15°C, within ±3.0 kg/m³ of the invoice density. That step in MDG does not mention the range {{1.2(iv)}}.
- **Every morning and after every load:** record your own density in the register {{1.4.1(c)}} {{1.2(xiv)}}.
- **At an inspection:** samples from your outlet are compared with your own recorded density {{2.10}}. A mobile lab report prints a largest gap of 3.0 kg/m³ between your tank's density after the last receipt and the density the officer reads {{annexure 12a}} {{annexure 13a}}.

**For example (an illustration with made-up figures):** the last density you recorded for your petrol tank after a load is 744.0. A mobile lab officer reads 759.0 at the nozzle. The reading of 759.0 lies inside 720–775. It is also 15.0 above your record, against a printed largest gap of 3.0. Being inside the range does not remove that gap from the report.

> **Note:** the mobile lab report carries both the range and the gap line. MDG does not say in words what follows when the gap is above the printed largest figure. Ask your oil company how it treats that line.

## Why density changes with temperature

This is a general explanation, not MDG's wording. Fuel swells as it warms, so a litre then weighs less, and a hydrometer reads lower on a hot day and higher on a cold morning. That is why every limit above is stated at 15°C, and why you note the temperature of each sample.

Read the hydrometer, read the thermometer, then convert to 15°C with the ASTM density conversion table that MDG requires you to hold {{1.5(vi)}}. MDG does not print that table, and we do not give a conversion here. The [15°C calculator](@tools-density-at-15) converts a reading and a temperature for you.

## Diesel from Assam crude

A footnote to the diesel figure of 810–845 says that whether the density range is relaxed for fuel made from Assam crude, and for how long, may be guided by Government of India notifications issued from time to time {{7.6}}. MDG gives no relaxed figure. If your supply may be affected, ask your oil company which range applies to you.
